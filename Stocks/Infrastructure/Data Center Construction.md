## Overview

The shell and the site: land, power rights, water rights, permitting, structural materials, and — critically — the human labor to install everything the other layers manufacture.

This layer is where the entire buildout ultimately converges. Every turbine, transformer, CDU, and rack must be physically installed by a licensed tradesperson on a permitted site. Microsoft's president has called the electrician shortage the single biggest bottleneck slowing datacenter expansion, which is a remarkable statement from a company whose constraint is nominally silicon.

## Demand

- ~23 GW of IT capacity under construction, ~75% US
- Datacenter contractors carry an average backlog of nearly **11 months**
- Construction labor costs in primary North American markets up **8–12% YoY**, almost entirely from skilled trades scarcity ([Build.inc](https://build.inc/insights/data-center-construction-labor-shortage-2026))
- Demand is geographically concentrated in a handful of markets, which makes local labor and utility conditions decisive rather than national averages

## Supply

### Skilled Labor — the Hard Constraint

- Projected shortfall of up to **499,000 workers** in datacenter construction; ~340,000 unfilled positions by end-2026
- **300,000+ new electricians** needed for AI-driven datacenter demand alone
- 45% of contractors reported at least one project delayed by staffing in the past year
- Qualification standards eliminate ~85% of applicants

The scarcity is not general construction labor. It is specifically licensed electricians, high-voltage field technicians, mechanical contractors, controls specialists, and commissioning engineers. An electrician requires roughly a four-year apprenticeship — the pipeline cannot be accelerated by price in under half a decade, and it cannot readily be imported because licensing is state-level.

This is the most durable bottleneck in the entire chain. Steel can be rolled faster. People cannot be trained faster.

### Materials

Structural steel, cement and concrete (huge volumes for slab and foundations given equipment weight), rebar, roofing, and precast panels. None of these are individually scarce, but datacenter construction competes for the same regional batch plants and fabricators as everything else being built. Aluminum and copper feed in from [[AI Critical Materials]].

Modular and prefabricated construction — skids, prefab power modules, containerised plant — is the main industry response. It moves work from the constrained site into a factory where less-scarce labor can perform it. This is a structural shift worth tracking.

### Land, Power Rights, Water Rights

Site selection now optimises for, in rough order: available power (or a credible BTM path), firm gas transport, water or a closed-loop design, fiber, then land cost. Land itself is rarely the binding item. Power availability is.

### Permitting and Local Politics

Increasingly the gating item in mature markets. Moratoria, zoning fights, and water-use objections have all materially delayed projects. Politically this is the softest attack surface on the buildout and the fastest-moving risk.

### Supply Shocks
- Immigration or labor policy changes affecting the construction workforce
- Tariffs on structural steel and aluminum
- A local moratorium cascade in a major market (Virginia, Texas, Arizona, Ohio)
- Utility refusal or deferral of new large-load interconnections — see [[Grid and Electrical Equipment]]

## Industry Type
- Cyclicality: High, but with unusually long visibility given 11-month backlogs and multi-year campus programmes.
- Capital Intensity: Low for contractors (people and equipment), extremely high for developers and owners. Very different business models sit under the same "construction" label — worth separating carefully.

## Risks
- **Labor cost inflation compresses contractor margins** even as revenue grows, if contracts are fixed-price. Contract structure is the whole question here.
- Backlog quality — signed does not mean funded or permitted.
- Efficiency gains in construction methods (modular, prefab) reduce site labor hours per MW, which cuts both the bottleneck and the revenue pool.
- A capex pause hits this layer with a lag but very hard, because contractors carry fixed crews they cannot easily shed.
- Reputational and political risk concentrated here — this is the visible, local face of the buildout.
